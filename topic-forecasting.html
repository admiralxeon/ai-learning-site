Forecasting: predict what comes next

This short topic is written in ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English. Read Module 3, Machine learning and Module 7, AI in practice first. Shops must know how many mangoes to order. Power companies must know how much electricity people will use tomorrow. This topic tells you how forecasts work, how to test them, and why a range is better than one number.

1

Scope

A forecast predicts future values from past values. This topic gives a description of the parts of a time series, baselines, features and models, the correct test of a forecast, and ranges of uncertainty.

2

The parts of a time series

A time series is a sequence of measurements in time order, for example the sales of each month. Most time series have three parts. Refer to Figure 1.

  • Trend: the slow, long-term increase or decrease. The farm sells a little more each year.
  • Seasonality: a pattern that repeats at the same time each day, week, or year. Mango sales have a peak each April to June.
  • Noise: the random changes that nobody can predict.

The number of steps into the future is the forecast horizon. A forecast for tomorrow is usually much more accurate than a forecast for next year.

3

Start with a baseline

Simple forecasts are frequently difficult to beat. Always calculate them first. Refer to Module 7, Section 4.

BaselineForecastGood when
Last valueThe same as the last measurement.The values change slowly, for example the temperature in the next hour.
Same time last seasonThe value of the same month last year, or the same day last week.There is a strong season, for example mango sales.
Moving averageThe mean of the last few values, for example the last 4 weeks.There is much noise and no strong season.
4

Features and models

Features

To use usual ML models, change the time series into a table. Each row is one time step. Useful features are:

  • Lag features: the values of the last steps, for example the sales of last week and of the same week last year.
  • Calendar: the day of the week, the month, holidays, and festivals such as Diwali.
  • Other known values: the price, a planned discount, the weather forecast.
Caution

Use only values that are known at the time of the forecast. The real weather of next week is not known today. Only the weather forecast is known. Otherwise this is data leakage. Refer to Module 7, Section 3.

Models

ModelHow it works
Exponential smoothingA weighted mean of the past. New values have more weight. It can also model trend and season.
ARIMAA classic statistical model that uses the past values and the past errors.
Gradient boostingMany decision trees, trained on the table with lag and calendar features. Often very good for sales.
Neural networksGood when there are thousands of related series, for example the sales of each product in each shop. New pretrained models for time series also exist.
5

Test a forecast correctly

Do not divide a time series at random. A random division puts future values into the training set. Always train on the past and test on the future.

Procedure: backtesting

  1. Select a date in the past, for example 1 January 2025.
  2. Train the model only with the data before that date.
  3. Make a forecast for the next steps, for example 3 months.
  4. Compare it with what really occurred.
  5. Move the date forward one month, and do steps 2 to 4 again. Calculate the mean error of all the tests.

We call this backtesting. It is the cross-validation of time series. Refer to Module 7, Section 5.

Measure the error

MonthRealForecastErrorError as % of real
April1201002016.7%

June901102022.2%
  • MAE (mean absolute error) = (20 + 10 + 20) ÷ 3 = 16.7 boxes. It is in the unit of the data.
  • MAPE (mean absolute percentage error) = (16.7% + 6.7% + 22.2%) ÷ 3 = 15.2%. It lets you compare series of different sizes. Do not use it when real values can be 0.
6

Give a range, not one number

No forecast is exact. A prediction interval gives a range with a probability. Example: "Next May: 140 boxes. With 80% probability, between 115 and 165 boxes."

The range helps people to make decisions:

  • Mangoes spoil quickly. If too many are left, the farm loses money. If there are too few, it loses customers. The range shows the risk on each side.
  • The range becomes wider further into the future. Plan again when new data comes.
Note

Check the range with backtesting too. If you say "80%", then approximately 80% of the real values must be in the ranges.

7

Where people use forecasting

AreaForecastUsual horizon
Shops and farmsDemand for each product, to order the correct quantity.Days to months
ElectricityPower use, and power from sun and wind.Hours to days
WeatherTemperature, rain, storms. New AI weather models now compete with physics models.Hours to 2 weeks
Hospitals and call centersThe number of patients or calls, to plan the staff.Hours to weeks
TransportTraffic and travel time, the number of passengers.Minutes to months
FinanceCash flow of a company. Prices of shares are very difficult to forecast.Weeks to years
8

Limits of forecasting

  • Sudden events. A pandemic, a strike, a flood, or a new law changes the pattern. A model that learned from the past cannot know this. A person must adjust the forecast.
  • Too little history. To learn a yearly season, you need at least two or three years of data.
  • The forecast changes the future. If the shop orders less because of a low forecast, it also sells less. The data then seems to agree with the forecast.
  • Records of sales are not demand. When a product is sold out, the sales show 0, but people still wanted it.
9

Summary

  • A time series has a trend, a season, and noise.
  • Compare each model with simple baselines: the last value, the same time last season, a moving average.
  • Lag and calendar features change a series into a table for usual ML models.
  • Never divide a time series at random. Use backtesting: train on the past, test on the future.
  • MAE is in the unit of the data. MAPE is a percentage.
  • Give a prediction interval, and adjust for sudden events.

11

Technical names

Backtesting
A test of a forecast method on past data: train on the data before a date, forecast after it, and compare. Repeat for many dates.
Forecast
A prediction of future values of a time series.
Forecast horizon
How far into the future a forecast predicts.
MAE
Mean absolute error: the mean of the sizes of the errors, in the unit of the data.
MAPE
Mean absolute percentage error: the mean of the errors as a percentage of the real values.
Prediction interval
A range in which the real value will probably be, with a stated probability.
Seasonality
A pattern that repeats at the same time each day, week, or year.
Time series
A sequence of measurements in time order.
Trend
The slow, long-term increase or decrease of a time series.
