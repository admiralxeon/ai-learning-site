ML system design: from a problem to a full design

This module is written in ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English, at approximately 80% compliance. Read Module 7, Module 8, and Module 9 first. Those modules give you the parts. This module tells you how to put the parts together into one design, before you write code. It gives a method, three worked case studies, and lessons from real failures. Interviews for ML jobs frequently ask for this type of design. Names with a dotted line are technical names. Put the pointer on a name to see its definition.

After this module, you can:
  • Design an ML system in seven steps, from requirements to risks.
  • Estimate the load, the latency budget, the storage, and the cost of a design.
  • Select a decision threshold from the costs of the two types of error.
  • Explain the lessons of real failures, and answer a design interview question.

Scope

This module tells you:

  • A method with seven steps to design an ML system.
  • How to estimate the load, the latency, and the cost with simple calculations.
  • How to select a decision threshold from the cost of each type of error.
  • Three full designs: fraud detection, recommendations, and a support chatbot.
  • What three real failures teach about ML systems.
  • How to answer a system design question in an interview.
2

A method in seven steps

A good design starts with questions, not with a model. Do the steps in this order. Refer to Figure 1.

StepQuestions to ask
1. RequirementsWho uses it? How many requests? How fast must the answer come? What occurs when it is wrong? Is AI the correct tool? Refer to Module 6, Section 7.
2. MetricsA business metric (money saved, time saved), a model metric (precision, recall), and guardrail metrics (latency, complaints, fairness).
3. DataWhere do the inputs and the labels come from? How late do the labels arrive? Is there personal data? Is there leakage?
4. ModelWhat is the baseline? What is the simplest model that can work? What are the features?
5. ServingBatch, online, or on the device? What is the latency budget? What is the cost of each prediction?
6. MonitoringHow do you know that it still works? Where do new labels come from? When do you train again?
7. RisksWho can be harmed? How can people abuse it? What is the fallback when it fails? Which trade-offs did you make?
3

Estimate load, latency, and cost

Simple calculations show early if a design can work. We call them back-of-the-envelope estimates.

Load

2,000,000 requests each day ÷ 86,400 seconds ≈ 23 requests each second (mean)

Traffic is not equal during the day. Plan for a peak of 3 to 5 times the mean: approximately 70 to 115 requests each second.

Latency budget

If the answer must come in 100 ms, divide the 100 ms between the parts. Example: network 30 ms, get the features 25 ms, model 25 ms, rules and logging 20 ms. If the model needs 200 ms, the design must change: a smaller model, a cache, or a prediction in advance (batch).

Storage and cost

  • A log of 1 KB for each request: 2,000,000 × 1 KB = 2 GB each day, approximately 60 GB each month.
  • An LLM answer that costs $0.018 (refer to Module 9, Section 9): 10,000 answers each day cost $180 each day, approximately $5,400 each month. Is the value larger than this?
4

Case study: fraud detection for card payments

The problem. A payment company must stop fraud in card payments. It processes 100,000 payments each day. Approximately 0.2% are fraud: 200 payments. Each decision must come in less than 100 ms, while the customer waits.

StepDesign decisions
MetricsMoney lost to fraud, good payments blocked, and the number of reviews each day. Accuracy is useless: "never fraud" is 99.8% accurate.
DataLabels from chargebacks arrive 30 to 90 days later. Thus the newest data has no labels yet. Divide the data by date, not at random.
FeaturesCompared with the history of the card: the amount compared with its usual amount, the number of payments in the last hour, a new device, a new country.
ModelBaseline: rules that experts write. Then gradient boosting on the features. It is fast, it works well on tables, and the important features can be explained.
ServingOnline, under 100 ms. The history of each card is kept in a fast store, updated after each payment.
MonitoringFraud changes quickly, because criminals adapt (concept drift). Measure the score distribution each day, and train again each week.
RisksBlocking good payments of some groups more than others. A blocked customer must have a fast way to get help.

Select the threshold from the costs

The model gives a score. The threshold decides which payments are blocked. The best threshold is not 0.5. It depends on the cost of each type of error. Refer to Module 3, Section 10.

total cost = missed fraud × cost of one missed fraud + blocked good payments × cost of one blocked payment

Try it

Find the cheapest threshold

One day: 100,000 payments, of which 200 are fraud. Move the threshold, and look at the total cost. Then change the two costs. How does the cheapest threshold move? All numbers are only examples.

5

Case study: recommendations for a shop

The problem. An online shop for fruit and vegetables has 2 million users and 50,000 products. The home page must show 20 products for each user. Refer to the short topic Recommendation systems.

StepDesign decisions
MetricsPurchases from the recommendations and the value of each order. Guardrails: returns, complaints, and the share of new products shown. Clicks alone can reward products that disappoint.
DataViews, carts, and purchases. Divide by date. A purchase is a stronger signal than a view.
ModelBaseline: the most popular products in the region. Then candidates from embeddings, and a ranking model.
ServingCalculate the candidates for each user each night (batch). Rank them online when the page opens, with the current cart and the time of day.
MonitoringA/B tests for each change. Season changes the demand: mangoes in May, not in December.
RisksFeedback loops, cold start for new products and users, and privacy of the purchase history.

A key trade-off: batch candidates are cheap and fast, but they do not know what the user did 5 minutes ago. The online ranking step adds that.

6

Case study: a support chatbot

The problem. A company gets 5,000 support messages each day in English, Hindi, and Marathi. It wants an assistant that answers the simple questions and sends the others to people. Refer to Module 9.

StepDesign decisions
MetricsThe share of questions solved without a person, the share of correct answers (from an evaluation set and a sample checked by people), and customer satisfaction. Guardrail: wrong answers about money or safety must be near zero.
DataHelp pages and policies for RAG, and past conversations to make the evaluation set. Remove personal data first.
ModelAn LLM with RAG and tools: order status, refund request. Baseline: a search of the help pages that shows the best page.
ServingStreaming answers. A clear way to talk to a person at each step. Refunds need human approval. Refer to Module 9, Section 7.
MonitoringCost and latency for each conversation, the share sent to people, new types of questions, and complaints.
RisksFalse answers, prompt injection, private data in logs, and worse quality in Hindi and Marathi. Test each language separately.

A key trade-off: a larger model gives better answers but costs more for each conversation. Measure both with the evaluation set, and use the larger model only where the smaller one fails.

7

Lessons from real failures

These systems were made by capable teams with much data. They failed because of the design around the model.

A hiring tool that learned old bias (Amazon)

In 2018, Reuters reported that Amazon stopped an experimental tool that scored job applications. The tool learned from approximately 10 years of past applications, and most of them came from men. It gave lower scores to applications that contained the word "women's", for example "women's chess club captain". The team could not be sure that it was fair, and the company stopped the project.

Lesson: past decisions are not correct labels. If the history contains bias, the model learns it. Measure the results for each group before use. Refer to Module 6, Section 3.

A price model that bought houses (Zillow Offers)

Zillow used price predictions to buy houses directly, and to sell them again. In 2021, prices changed quickly, and the company bought many houses for more than it could sell them for. In November 2021, it closed the program, reported a write-down of approximately 300 million US dollars for the quarter, and planned to reduce its staff by approximately 25%.

Lesson: when the model's predictions decide large purchases, small errors become very expensive. The market changed faster than the model (drift). Use ranges of uncertainty, limits on risk, and fast monitoring. Refer to Forecasting, Section 6.

A flu forecast from searches (Google Flu Trends)

Google Flu Trends estimated flu activity from search queries. In the 2012 to 2013 season, it estimated approximately double the level that the US public health agency (CDC) measured. Researchers found that the model followed search terms that were only correlated with flu, and that changes in search behavior moved the estimates. Google stopped publishing the estimates in 2015.

Lesson: correlation is not cause, and the meaning of a signal can change. Compare with a simple baseline and with the real measurements all the time. Refer to Module 2, Section 6.

8

Design interviews

In an ML system design interview, you get an open problem, for example "Design the feed of a video app", and approximately 45 minutes. The interviewer looks at your method more than at the final answer.

Procedure: answer a design question

  1. Clarify (5 minutes). Ask about the users, the scale, the latency, and the cost of errors. Write the answers.
  2. Metrics (5 minutes). Say the business metric, the model metric, and the guardrail metrics.
  3. Data and features (10 minutes). Say where the labels come from, and how late they arrive. Name the main features.
  4. Model (10 minutes). Start with a baseline. Then a model with a reason. Do not start with the most complex model.
  5. Serving and monitoring (10 minutes). Batch or online, the latency budget, and how you find drift.
  6. Risks and trade-offs (5 minutes). Say what you gave up, and why. Name the risks for users.

Practice questions

  • Design a system that finds damaged mangoes on a sorting line with a camera.
  • Design the search of an online shop.
  • Design a spam filter for the messages of a social app.
  • Design a forecast of the demand for each product in 500 shops.
  • Design an assistant that answers questions about the rules of a college.

For each question, write a design with the table of the case studies above. Then compare it with a friend's design.

9

Summary

  • Design in seven steps: requirements, metrics, data, model, serving, monitoring, and risks.
  • Use back-of-the-envelope estimates for load, latency, storage, and cost.
  • Select a threshold from the costs of the two types of error, not 0.5.
  • Labels can arrive late. Divide data by date, and plan how new labels come back.
  • Start with a baseline. Add complexity only when the metrics show the need.
  • Real failures came from biased labels, drift, and correlations that changed, not from the code.

11

Technical names

These names are not in the STE dictionary. STE lets you use technical names that are necessary for the subject.

Back-of-the-envelope estimate
A quick calculation with round numbers that shows if a design is possible.
Chargeback
A payment that the bank of the card holder reverses, for example after fraud. It is a late label for fraud detection.
Guardrail metric
A metric that must not become worse when the main metric improves.
Latency budget
The maximum time for an answer, divided between the parts of a system.
Peak load
The highest number of requests in a short time, usually 3 to 5 times the mean.
System design
The plan of all the parts of a system and how they work together, before it is built.
Threshold
The score above which a model's prediction becomes an action, for example a block.
