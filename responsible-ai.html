Responsible AI: general description

This module is written in ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English, at approximately 80% compliance. Read Modules 1 to 5 before you read this module. This module tells you how to use AI in a fair, safe, and legal way. It ends with a project in which you plan your own AI solution. Names with a dotted line are technical names. Put the pointer on a name to see its definition.

After this module, you can:
  • Find and measure bias in a model.
  • Apply the basic rules of privacy and AI law.
  • Plan explanations and human oversight.
  • Evaluate an AI tool, and decide when not to use AI.

2

What responsible AI is

An AI system can help many people. It can also cause harm. For example, it can refuse a loan to a person without a good reason, or it can show private data. Responsible AI is the work to prevent this harm.

Most companies and governments use the same principles:

PrincipleWhat it meansExample question
FairnessThe system works equally well for all groups of people.Does the system make more errors for women, older people, or people from one region?
TransparencyPeople know that AI is used, and they know what it does.Do customers know that a chatbot is not a person?
PrivacyThe system uses personal data only with permission, and it keeps the data safe.Do we need the name of the customer, or only the age?
SafetyThe system does not cause physical or financial harm.What occurs when the system is wrong?
AccountabilityA person or an organization is responsible for each decision.Who answers the complaint of a customer?
Human oversightA person can check, change, or stop the decisions.Can a worker stop the system immediately?
Note

The AI does not have responsibility. People do. The organization that uses the system is responsible for its decisions.

3

Bias and fairness

An AI system has bias when it makes more errors for some groups than for others. Bias can get into the system at each step of the ML workflow. Refer to Figure 1.

Real examples

  • Face recognition. In 2018, the Gender Shades study tested face analysis systems from large companies. The systems made far more errors for women with dark skin than for men with light skin. The training data contained mostly photographs of men with light skin.
  • Hiring. In 2018, news reports told that a large company stopped an AI hiring tool. The tool gave lower scores to CVs that contained the word "women's". It learned from ten years of old hiring decisions.

Procedure: find bias

  1. Write down the groups that the system can treat differently, for example by gender, age, region, or language.
  2. Make sure that the test data has enough examples of each group.
  3. Measure the accuracy, precision, and recall for each group separately. Refer to Module 3, Section 10.
  4. Compare the results. A large difference between groups is a sign of bias.
  5. If you find bias, collect more data for the weak groups, change the design, or do not use the system for these groups.

Project: the mango checker · Part 6 of 9

Is the checker fair?

Asha sells her mango checker to farmers in Gujarat. They grow Kesar mangoes. After one week, they complain: the checker puts many good Kesar mangoes into the damaged box.

The team looks at the training data. 95% of the photographs show Alphonso mangoes from Asha's farm. Kesar mangoes have different natural marks on the skin. The model thinks that these marks are damage.

The team does these steps:

  • They measure the recall for each type of mango separately.
  • They tell the farmers in Gujarat to stop using the checker until the problem is repaired.
  • They collect 2,000 photographs of Kesar mangoes and train the model again.
  • Now each new type of mango gets its own test before farmers use the checker.
Think: why did the test in Part 3 not show this problem?

The test set came from Asha's farm. It contained almost no Kesar mangoes. A test must contain examples of each group that will use the system.

4

Privacy and the law

Many AI systems use personal data. Personal data is any data that can identify a person, for example a name, a phone number, a photograph of a face, or a location.

Laws about personal data

  • India. The DPDP Act (Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023) tells organizations how to collect and use personal data. They must get permission for a clear purpose, keep the data safe, and let people correct or delete their data.
  • European Union. The GDPR has been used since 2018. It also gives people rights about automatic decisions that have a large effect on them.

Follow the principle of data minimization: collect only the data that the system really needs. Data that you do not have cannot be stolen.

Laws about AI

The European Union made the first large law about AI: the EU AI Act (2024). It puts AI systems into four levels of risk. A higher risk has stricter rules. Refer to Figure 2.

Caution

Laws are different in each country, and they change frequently. Before you use AI with personal data or for important decisions, get advice from a legal expert.

5

Explanations and human oversight

Module 4 tells you that it is difficult to know why a deep network gives an answer. But when a decision has a large effect on a person, the person must be able to ask "why?". Explainability is the ability to answer this question.

Some methods help:

  • Show the most important features. For example: "The loan was refused mainly because of the low income and the short work history."
  • Show similar examples. For example: "Mangoes with these marks were damaged in 9 of 10 cases."
  • Use a simpler model. For important decisions, a decision tree can be better than a deep network, because people can read it.

Human oversight

There are two common designs:

DesignHow it worksUse it when
Human in the loopThe AI suggests. A person approves each decision.Each error has a large effect, for example a medical diagnosis or a loan.
Human on the loopThe AI decides. A person monitors the results and can stop the system.There are many small decisions, for example spam filters or sorting mangoes.
Warning

A human check is useful only if the person has the time, the knowledge, and the authority to disagree with the AI. If a person always accepts the AI's suggestion, there is no real check.

6

Evaluate an AI tool before you trust it

Many companies sell AI tools. Their demonstrations show the best results. Do your own test with your own data.

Procedure: evaluate an AI tool

  1. Write the task, and write what a good result is.
  2. Collect 20 to 50 real examples from your task. Include difficult examples.
  3. Test the tool on these examples. Count the errors, and look at each error.
  4. Compare the results for different groups of users.
  5. Read where the tool keeps your data, and if the company uses your data for training.
  6. Decide who checks the results, and who is responsible for errors.
  7. Start with a small, careful test. Monitor the results before you use the tool widely.

7

When not to use AI

AI is not always the best solution. A usual program, a simple rule, or a person can be better. Do not use AI in these cases:

  • A simple rule works. If a person can write clear rules that are always correct, use a usual program. It is cheaper and easier to check.
  • There is no good data. Without enough correct and fair examples, the model cannot learn.
  • Nobody can check the errors. If an error can cause serious harm and no person can check the decisions, do not use AI.
  • The law requires an explanation that you cannot give.
  • The task needs human care. For example, telling a patient bad news.
  • The cost is larger than the benefit. Include the cost of data, training, checks, and errors.
Note

"Do not use AI" is a correct result of a careful plan. It is not a failure.

9

Summary

  • Responsible AI is fair, transparent, private, safe, and under human control. People are responsible, not the AI.
  • Bias can get into a system through the data, the labels, the design, or the use.
  • Measure the results for each group separately to find bias.
  • Laws such as the DPDP Act, the GDPR, and the EU AI Act control personal data and AI.
  • For important decisions, give explanations and let a qualified person check each decision.
  • Test an AI tool with your own examples before you trust it.
  • Sometimes the correct decision is not to use AI.

11

Technical names

These names are not in the STE dictionary. STE lets you use technical names that are necessary for the subject.

Accountability
A person or an organization is responsible for the results of an AI system.
Bias
A systematic error that makes a system treat some groups worse than others.
Data minimization
The principle to collect and keep only the personal data that a system really needs.
DPDP Act
India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023.
EU AI Act
The European Union law about AI, adopted in 2024. It has four levels of risk.
Explainability
The ability to tell people why an AI system made a decision.
Fairness
The system works equally well for all groups of people.
GDPR
General Data Protection Regulation: the EU law about personal data.
Human in the loop
A design in which a person approves each decision before it has an effect.
Human oversight
People can check, change, or stop the decisions of an AI system.
Personal data
Data that can identify a person, for example a name, a face, or a location.
Transparency
People know when AI is used and how it works.
