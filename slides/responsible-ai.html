What responsible AI is

An AI system can help many people. It can also cause harm. For example, it can refuse a loan to a person without a good reason, or it can show private data. Responsible AI is the work to prevent this harm.

Notes: Most companies and governments use the same principles: The AI does not have responsibility. People do. The organization that uses the system is responsible for its decisions. Open the section

Privacy and the law

Many AI systems use personal data. Personal data is any data that can identify a person, for example a name, a phone number, a photograph of a face, or a location.

Notes: Follow the principle of data minimization : collect only the data that the system really needs. Data that you do not have cannot be stolen. The European Union made the first large law about AI: the EU AI Act (2024). It puts AI systems into four levels of risk. A higher risk has stricter rules. Refer to Figure 2. Laws are different in each country, and they change frequently. Before you use AI with personal data or for important decisions, get advice from a legal expert. Open the section

Explanations and human oversight

Module 4 tells you that it is difficult to know why a deep network gives an answer. But when a decision has a large effect on a person, the person must be able to ask "why?". Explainability is the ability to answer this question.

  • Show the most important features. For example: "The loan was refused mainly because of the low income and the short work history."
  • Show similar examples. For example: "Mangoes with these marks were damaged in 9 of 10 cases."
  • Use a simpler model. For important decisions, a decision tree can be better than a deep network, because people can read it.
Notes: Some methods help: There are two common designs: A human check is useful only if the person has the time, the knowledge, and the authority to disagree with the AI. If a person always accepts the AI's suggestion, there is no real check. Open the section

Evaluate an AI tool before you trust it

Many companies sell AI tools. Their demonstrations show the best results. Do your own test with your own data.

  1. Write the task, and write what a good result is.
  2. Collect 20 to 50 real examples from your task. Include difficult examples.
  3. Test the tool on these examples. Count the errors, and look at each error.
  4. Compare the results for different groups of users.
  5. Read where the tool keeps your data, and if the company uses your data for training.
  6. Decide who checks the results, and who is responsible for errors.

When not to use AI

AI is not always the best solution. A usual program, a simple rule, or a person can be better. Do not use AI in these cases:

  • A simple rule works. If a person can write clear rules that are always correct, use a usual program. It is cheaper and easier to check.
  • There is no good data. Without enough correct and fair examples, the model cannot learn.
  • Nobody can check the errors. If an error can cause serious harm and no person can check the decisions, do not use AI.
  • The law requires an explanation that you cannot give.
  • The task needs human care. For example, telling a patient bad news.
  • The cost is larger than the benefit. Include the cost of data, training, checks, and errors.
Notes: "Do not use AI" is a correct result of a careful plan. It is not a failure. Open the section

Summary

  • Responsible AI is fair, transparent, private, safe, and under human control. People are responsible, not the AI.
  • Bias can get into a system through the data, the labels, the design, or the use.
  • Measure the results for each group separately to find bias.
  • Laws such as the DPDP Act, the GDPR, and the EU AI Act control personal data and AI.
  • For important decisions, give explanations and let a qualified person check each decision.
  • Test an AI tool with your own examples before you trust it.
